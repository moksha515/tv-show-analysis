    Which shows get renewed?

    We predict renewal from IMDb rating, vote count, network, genre, and season 1 episode count. The model returns a renewal probability for every show. Each chart below comes with a short note on what is happening in it.

    Bar plots: renewal rate by genre and by network

    What is happening
    Each bar is the share of shows in that group that got renewed. One chart groups shows by genre and the other by network.
    Why it matters
    It shows what the renewal rate looks like across networks and genres, and the patterns between a genre and a network. Those patterns feed into the overall renewal probability.
    How we check it
    A chi-square test compares groups, for example P(renewed | Netflix) against P(renewed | CBS), to see whether the gap is real or just chance.

    The logistic regression model: how the probability is made

    The model learns from past shows what predicts renewal, then gives a renewal probability for each show.

    1. History

      The model is given past data: rating, votes, network, genre, season 1 episode count, and whether the show was renewed or cancelled.

    2. Weights

      It starts with a 50% guess, checks how wrong the guesses are, and adds up all the mistakes (the loss). Then it nudges the weights to lower the loss and repeats until improvement slows down. Every show gets a renewal percentage, even the cancelled ones, so a high percentage on a cancelled show counts as a mistake.

    3. Score a show

      Each clue is multiplied by its weight, and the results are added up. A high score points to renewal and a low score points to cancellation. For example, if high-rated shows were renewed more than predicted, the weight on rating goes up.

    4. Score to percentage

      The score is passed through the logistic function, which squeezes it into a percentage between 0% and 100%.

    The simulator and the "Pitch a new show" card use this percentage as the renewal chance each season, and repeat the process thousands of times to estimate how long a show will last.

    ROC curve: can the model tell renewed from cancelled?

    What is happening
    After the logistic regression model is built, this curve shows how well it can tell renewed shows apart from cancelled ones.
    Why it matters
    It is a measure of the model's accuracy in its predictions. The closer the curve gets to the top-left corner, the better the model. A diagonal line would mean it is no better than guessing.

    Calibration plot: can we believe the percentages?

    What is happening
    This checks whether the predicted chances are believable by comparing the model's percentage to the actual percentage of shows that were renewed.
    Why it matters
    If shows given about 70% really are renewed about 70% of the time, the points sit on the diagonal and the probabilities can be trusted in the simulator.

    1. Get each prediction

      The model gives every show a predicted renewal percentage.

    2. Group into buckets

      Shows with similar predictions are grouped together, such as 0 to 10%, 10 to 20%, up to 90 to 100%.

    3. Calculate the real rate

      For each bucket, count how many of its shows were actually renewed (season 1 to season 2).

    4. Plot it

      Predicted percentage goes on one axis and the real renewal rate on the other.

    How long can a show last?

    Renewal odds change with every season. We use them to simulate thousands of show lifetimes, then read off the chance of reaching 5, 7, or 10 seasons.

    Do shows get worse over time?

    Only shows with more than one season, and mostly those that kept getting renewed. We compare season 1 to the final season and measure how often ratings drop.
